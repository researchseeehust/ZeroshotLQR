Reward-free data, once. Any LQR task, instantly.

Classical RL and adaptive dynamic programming for the linear quadratic regulator solve one performance index at a time: change the weights $(Q,R)$ and the whole policy iteration must be rerun on stored, or freshly collected, data. We bring zero-shot reinforcement learning to LQR — a fixed-size statistic of reward-free data solves every task afterwards, exactly, without touching the data again.

Full abstract

For linear systems with quadratic costs, the successor features of every linear policy are exact quadratic forms, and a fixed-size second-moment statistic of reward-free, off-policy data is a sufficient representation to solve every task. An offline phase compresses arbitrary trajectories into this statistic and, optionally, a small library of successor operators for anchor tasks. At test time, any task — revealed as weights $(Q,R)$ or through noisy reward labels — is mapped to a stabilizing gain by a data-free policy iteration, or with zero iterations by successor-feature generalized policy improvement. Under sub-Gaussian process noise, the returned gains are stabilizing and their relative suboptimality is bounded uniformly over a compact task set, with no union bound over tasks. We validate the approach on Rosenbrock's open-loop-unstable batch reactor.

Theorem — uniform zero-shot guarantee

Under sub-Gaussian process noise, with probability at least $1-\delta$ — from a single probabilistic event, with no union bound over tasks — the gains returned by Mode PI are stabilizing and their relative suboptimality is bounded, uniformly over a compact task set $\mathcal{W}$, by $O\!\big(\sigma_w\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/(N\lambda_{\mathrm{PE}})}\big)$. A data-computable certificate, evaluated from the representation alone, verifies stability and suboptimality for each individual task.

Machine-precision exactness, constant test-time cost, robust finite-sample rates.

A single reward-free dataset solves 200 random LQR tasks to machine precision; SF-GPI closes most of the optimality gap with no test-time equation solving; and the predicted $1/N$ sample-complexity rate is confirmed across three excitation levels.
